\begin{abstract}
Relation-based expensive many-objective optimization requires informative training
groups and effective candidate selection under limited evaluation budgets.
Penalty-based boundary intersection (PBI) grouping with a small
representative set can leave additional directional quality information unused.
Ranking candidates solely by relation scores also leaves classification ambiguity
and indicator information outside the selection rule. We propose PACDIS, a
surrogate-assisted evolutionary algorithm that combines PBI-assisted quality
classification (PAQC) with criterion-diversified infill selection (CDIS).
PAQC supplements representative-based labels with continuous PBI scores.
Both signals use a common estimated ideal point for direction construction,
association, and distance evaluation. Current nondominated solutions provide
the continuous-assessment directions, with uniform directions as a fallback.
A fusion weight that changes with evaluation progress
determines positive-group membership for relation learning.
CDIS first screens candidates by relation quality, then applies ambiguity-oriented
or indicator-oriented ranking to select an expensive-evaluation batch.
These modes use classification ambiguity and a separate quality-indicator
surrogate, respectively. Experiments compare PACDIS with six algorithms on
48 DTLZ and WFG configurations with 10, 15, and 20 objectives under a configured
budget of 500 evaluations. PACDIS achieves the lowest mean inverted generational
distance plus (IGD$^+$) in 15 configurations, including DTLZ6, WFG2, and WFG7
at all three objective counts. On four DTLZ problems,
matched population checkpoints show that PAQC more than doubles mean
current-convergence precision relative to equal-quota representative-margin
grouping (73.68\% versus 35.83\%).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% Stakes: expensive evaluations must support progress and multiple trade-offs.
Engineering applications such as airfoil design and power electronic circuit
optimization involve conflicting objectives whose evaluation can require costly
simulations~\cite{ref:gcs_pso,ref:emmoea}. The available evaluation budget limits
how many designs an optimizer can examine. In many-objective problems, this
restriction accompanies a selection difficulty: Pareto dominance provides less
discrimination as the number of objectives increases~\cite{ref:maoea_survey}.
Decomposition and reference-point methods help organize the search across
trade-offs~\cite{ref:moead,ref:nsga3}, but conventional evolutionary search still
requires objective evaluations for its new solutions. Expensive many-objective
optimization therefore requires selection rules that use scarce evaluations to
improve both convergence and the representation of different trade-offs.

% Research context: connect learning targets to the decisions they support.
Surrogate-assisted evolutionary algorithms (SAEAs) use predictions to screen
candidates before expensive evaluation~\cite{ref:jin_saea,ref:saea_survey}.
Learning a separate Kriging model for each objective makes the model count
grow with the number of objectives~\cite{ref:krvea}. Recent alternatives learn
convergence and diversity indicators, grid labels, or pairwise comparisons
that directly support selection~\cite{ref:hesea,ref:gcs_pso,ref:pcsaea}.
These targets offer different ways to summarize objective information. Their
usefulness depends on which quality distinctions the training data encode and
how the algorithm uses predictions to select the next evaluations.

% Nearest framework: distinguish inherited relation learning from our changes.
REMO, an evolutionary algorithm based on relation learning, connects these
decisions through a three-class relation model~\cite{ref:remo}.
It divides evaluated solutions into positive and
non-positive groups, learns relations between ordered pairs from these groups,
and aggregates predicted relations to score candidates. Group construction
therefore specifies the preferences that the model learns, while candidate
selection determines which predictions lead to new evaluations. The recent
relation-model framework of Hao et al. explicitly distinguishes data
preparation, model training, and model usage~\cite{ref:relation_framework}.
Within this established framework, we examine two decisions that govern the
use of objective information in expensive many-objective search.

% First motivation: limited representative directions can restrict supervision.
First, representative-based grouping derives its directional supervision from
a small set of selected solutions. REMO selects representatives with the radial
space division-based evolutionary algorithm (RSEA), which uses a two-dimensional
radial grid~\cite{ref:remo,ref:rsea}. REMO's penalty-based boundary intersection
(PBI) comparisons use the original objective vectors, with the representatives
providing the assessment directions. A limited representative set may leave
directions in the current nondominated population without a separate quality
assessment. Solutions that share a representative-based label can differ in
quality along these additional directions. REMO gives pairs within each group
the same relation label, so these differences do not directly enter its training
targets. This motivates using directional information from the current
nondominated population to refine training-group construction.

% Second motivation: quality scores leave additional infill priorities open.
Second, REMO's relation score provides a quality ranking for candidates, but it
does not assign a separate role to classification ambiguity or an additional
quality indicator in evaluation allocation~\cite{ref:remo}. A candidate's
predicted rank and the decisiveness of its class probabilities describe
different properties. Existing infill methods already consider several
criteria. The Kriging-assisted two-archive algorithm adapts sampling to
convergence, diversity, and uncertainty~\cite{ref:kta2}. Qin et al. select
evaluations through expected improvement in a performance
indicator~\cite{ref:emmoea}. For relation-based search,
these precedents motivate using additional selection criteria within a pool
that first passes a relation-quality screen.

% Design intuition: one overview, with established components acknowledged.
We develop PACDIS for expensive many-objective optimization. PACDIS revises
training-group construction and infill selection within REMO's relation-learning
framework (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}).
\emph{PBI-assisted quality classification} (PAQC) supplements representative-based
labels with PBI quality along directions derived from current nondominated
solutions relative to the current estimated ideal point when this construction
is available. Both assessment signals use this point as their common geometric
reference. A fusion that changes with
evaluation progress determines the positive training group.
\emph{Criterion-diversified infill selection} (CDIS) screens candidates by
relation quality, then selects an evaluation batch through one of two modes.
The ambiguity-oriented mode rewards less decisive relation predictions; the
indicator-oriented mode uses a surrogate of a quality indicator based on density
and front shape~\cite{ref:sde,ref:piea}. Each iteration selects one mode with a
fixed probability when the indicator model is available and uses the
ambiguity-oriented mode otherwise.

% Contributions: two concrete mechanisms, with experimental evidence below.
The two methodological contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item \textbf{Directional information for relation supervision.}
PAQC combines representative-based labels with a continuous directional quality
score to determine training-group membership. The additional assessment can
distinguish solutions that share the same representative-based label, while
the algorithm retains the three-class relation-model architecture.
\item \textbf{Alternative infill criteria after relation-quality screening.}
CDIS gives classification ambiguity and a separately learned quality indicator
distinct roles in selecting expensive evaluations. Both modes retain the
relation-quality screen, restricting their final rankings to candidates that
pass this initial assessment.
\end{enumerate}

% Results preview: descriptive means from the current FE500 table snapshot.
The experiments compare PACDIS with six SAEAs on 48 DTLZ and WFG configurations with 10,
15, and 20 objectives under a configured budget of 500 evaluations.
PACDIS achieves the lowest mean inverted generational distance plus (IGD$^+$)
in 15 configurations, including DTLZ6, WFG2, and WFG7 at all three
objective counts (Tables~\ref{tab:exp:dtlz} and~\ref{tab:exp:wfg}).
Separate experiments compare algorithm variants and examine the probability
of selecting each infill mode
(Sections~\ref{sec:exp:ablation} and~\ref{sec:exp:pmix}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Surrogate Targets for Expensive Optimization}
\label{sec:related:saea}

% Modeling targets: establish alternatives to objective-wise regression.
The choice of prediction target determines which information a surrogate
provides for evolutionary selection. Objective-wise regression retains the
individual trade-offs: K-RVEA trains one Kriging model per objective and uses
reference vectors to guide the search~\cite{ref:krvea}. The Kriging-assisted
two-archive algorithm (KTA2) also approximates individual objectives and
maintains archives that emphasize convergence and
diversity~\cite{ref:kta2}. Such approaches require more models as the number
of objectives grows, and selection must combine their predictions and
uncertainty estimates. This motivates learning a smaller set of quantities
that directly support selection~\cite{ref:hesea}.

% Indicator surrogates already address model reduction; acknowledge them.
Indicator surrogates compress objective information into measures of solution
quality. The hierarchical and ensemble surrogate-assisted evolutionary
algorithm (HES-EA) learns convergence and diversity indicators through pairs
of models within a clustering framework~\cite{ref:hesea}. A cluster surrogate
routes candidates to the corresponding indicator models. The performance indicator-based
evolutionary algorithm (PIEA) approximates an indicator and combines
its predictions with distribution information during
selection~\cite{ref:piea}. These methods already provide alternatives to
regressing every objective; the relevant distinction is which quality
information the learning target retains. Classification and relation models
make a further choice: they encode quality through discrete labels or
comparisons~\cite{ref:csea,ref:remo}.

\subsection{Supervision for Classification and Relation Learning}
\label{sec:related:relation}

% Different label constructions preserve different objective-space information.
Classification-based selection depends on the rule that assigns quality
labels to evaluated solutions. CSEA constructs labels from comparisons with
reference solutions and uses a neural network to classify new
candidates~\cite{ref:csea}. Yuan and Banzhaf's dominance-prediction approach
trains separate classifiers for Pareto dominance and $\theta$-dominance,
combining their predictions in a two-stage preselection
strategy~\cite{ref:theta_dea_dp}. Grid classification-based surrogate-assisted
particle swarm optimization (GCS-PSO) instead predicts objective-space grid
locations, from which it assesses convergence and
diversity~\cite{ref:gcs_pso}. The supervision rule therefore determines which
directional or distribution information a classifier can learn.

% Closest relation methods: separate group construction from model use.
REMO constructs pairwise supervision from two groups of evaluated
solutions~\cite{ref:remo}. It uses representative-based PBI comparisons to
form the groups, trains a three-class relation model on ordered solution
pairs, and aggregates predicted relations into candidate scores. PC-SAEA
constructs comparison labels using a fitness assessment that considers
convergence and diversity; it also validates the model before deciding how
to use its predictions~\cite{ref:pcsaea}. The recent relation-model framework
of Hao et al. explicitly separates data preparation, model training, and
model usage~\cite{ref:relation_framework}. Within this framework, changing
the quality information that defines training pairs is a distinct design
choice from changing the classifier or its use in selection.

% PAQC positioning: supplement representative-induced directions, with bounds.
RSEA uses radial projection and adaptive grids for selection, with both
crowding and convergence considered to address the effects of curvature
information loss~\cite{ref:rsea}. In REMO, the resulting representatives
retain their original objective vectors and provide the directions for
PBI-based grouping~\cite{ref:remo}. A limited representative set may leave
some directions in the current nondominated population without a separate
quality assessment. PAQC supplements this grouping signal with PBI quality
information along directions derived from current nondominated objective
vectors, with uniform-direction fallbacks when that construction is
unavailable. Its progress-dependent fusion determines positive-group
membership while retaining REMO's three-class relation model
(Section~\ref{sec:method}). The distinction from dominance prediction and
grid classification therefore lies in how PAQC combines two sources of
directional information to construct relation-training groups.

\subsection{Infill Criteria for Expensive Evaluations}
\label{sec:related:selection}

% Existing uncertainty and switching mechanisms preclude broad novelty claims.
Infill selection determines which surrogate predictions lead to new
expensive evaluations. KTA2 adapts its sampling strategy to the current need
for convergence, diversity, or uncertainty reduction~\cite{ref:kta2}, while
CSEA combines predicted class membership with classification uncertainty
when selecting candidates~\cite{ref:csea}. For objectives with different
evaluation latencies, the asynchronous influence-based evolutionary
algorithm (AIEA) combines candidate influence with a most-uncertain-first
rule for choosing the next objective to evaluate~\cite{ref:aiea}. These
methods already use uncertainty and multiple selection considerations, but
their decisions concern different prediction targets and evaluation
settings. PACDIS considers a batch of solutions for evaluation on all
objectives, using relation predictions as the initial quality screen.

% Indicator infill is a close precedent; distinguish acquisition quantities.
Indicator-based infill provides a further way to combine convergence and
diversity in evaluation allocation. Qin et al. search with
objective-wise Gaussian process models, then use a separate indicator
model to select the candidate with the largest expected improvement in
that indicator~\cite{ref:emmoea}. This separates candidate generation from
the criterion for expensive evaluation. PC-SAEA uses validation reliability
to choose between direct, reversed,
and suspended use of its comparison model~\cite{ref:pcsaea}. Expected
improvement, validation reliability, and ambiguity in a candidate's class
probabilities quantify different properties: predicted gain, model-level
correctness, and confidence in individual classifications, respectively.

% CDIS positioning: identify the concrete inherited and modified operations.
CDIS retains relation-guided candidate generation and applies relation-quality
screening before either of its final selection criteria. Its
ambiguity-oriented mode rewards candidates whose relation-class predictions
are less decisive; its indicator-oriented mode reranks candidates with a
surrogate of a quality indicator that uses shift-based density estimation
and front-shape information~\cite{ref:sde,ref:piea}. Each iteration selects
one mode with a fixed probability when the indicator model is available,
and otherwise uses the ambiguity-oriented mode. Thus, CDIS specifies how
classification ambiguity and indicator assessment allocate evaluations
within a relation-screened pool. Together, PAQC and CDIS modify the
supervision and evaluation-allocation rules of REMO, building on established
relation learning and indicator-based selection.

\section{The Proposed PACDIS}
\label{sec:method}

Relation-based surrogate-assisted evolutionary algorithms replace multi-output
regression by a classifier that predicts the relative quality of a pair of
solutions~\cite{ref:remo}. Two interfaces then decide what such an algorithm
can learn and how much its predictions cost: the rule that turns the evaluated
population into supervision labels, and the rule that turns the surrogate
scores of a large candidate pool into a small batch of expensive evaluations.
We propose PACDIS, a surrogate-assisted many-objective evolutionary
algorithm that modifies supervision construction through PAQC and
expensive-evaluation allocation through CDIS.
\emph{PBI-assisted quality classification} (PAQC) augments the
representative-based grouping of REMO~\cite{ref:remo} with PBI-based quality
information from directions constructed directly from the current nondominated
set relative to the estimated ideal point. The representative-based signal uses
the same point for association and distance evaluation. This additional source enriches the distribution information available for
relation-group construction. PAQC fuses the two signals to determine which
solutions enter the positive training group.
\emph{Criterion-diversified infill selection} (CDIS) retains relation-guided
candidate generation but uses either indicator-based reranking or
ambiguity-rewarded ranking to choose the final expensive-evaluation
batch. Established relation learning, indicator assessment and environmental
selection modules provide the host algorithm; the two proposed mechanisms
change how its training groups are formed and how its surrogate scores are used.

\subsection{Overall Framework}
\label{sec:method:framework}

We consider the expensive many-objective problem
$\min_{\vx\in\Omega}\vf(\vx)=[f_1(\vx),\ldots,f_M(\vx)]$,
where $\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}$ and every evaluation of $\vf$ is
expensive. The number of real function evaluations is therefore limited to a
budget $FE_{\max}$. Let $FE$ be the number of
evaluations consumed so far and $t=FE/FE_{\max}\in[0,1]$ the evaluation
progress, which controls the hybrid score in PAQC.

PACDIS evaluates a Latin hypercube design of $N_{\mathrm{init}}$ solutions,
stores them in an archive $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{rc}}$, and then repeats five
steps until the budget is exhausted. (i)~The current population $\mathcal{P}$
is divided into a positive group and a non-positive group by
PAQC, which also returns the set $\mathcal{R}$ of
representative solutions reused later as an additional mating pool.
(ii)~Ordered pairs are generated from the two groups and a three-class relation
network is trained using the resulting ordered group labels.
(iii)~An indicator value is computed for each solution in $\mathcal{P}$ and an
RBF-SVR model is fitted from decision vectors to those values.
(iv)~One of the two selection modes is drawn, a relation-guided inner search
accumulates candidates under that mode, and a batch of at most $n_{\max}$ candidates is
returned. (v)~The batch is evaluated, appended to
$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{rc}}$, and the next population is obtained by the
radial-grid environmental selection of RSEA~\cite{ref:rsea} over the whole
archive.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:framework} states the procedure; its grouping and candidate
selection steps are detailed in Algorithms~\ref{alg:paqc}
and~\ref{alg:cdis}, respectively. The initial design satisfies
$2\leq N_{\mathrm{init}}\leq FE_{\max}$, and $N$ is the target population size
for environmental selection. The relation surrogate $\mathcal{M}_R$ includes
the group references and input normalisation needed for prediction, while
$\mathcal{M}_I$ denotes the indicator surrogate when available. The controls
are shared by the three procedures.
All expensive evaluations
after initialisation are consumed in step~(v), so both proposed mechanisms operate on already
evaluated data and surrogate predictions only, and neither changes the total
number of real evaluations. The two interfaces stay separate: grouping
decides \emph{what the classifier is asked to learn}, candidate selection
decides \emph{how its scores are spent}.

Figure~\ref{fig:framework} shows the evaluation loop and expands CDIS into its
relation-guided candidate search and criterion-dependent infill branches.

\label{alg:framework}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\subsection{PBI-Assisted Quality Classification (PAQC)}
\label{sec:method:paqc}

Relation learning requires training groups that reflect the quality of evaluated
solutions. REMO~\cite{ref:remo} derives its grouping signal from a small set of
representatives selected through RSEA's two-dimensional radial grid~\cite{ref:rsea}.
These representatives retain their original objective vectors, but the projected
distribution guides their selection. This sparse summary may not fully reflect
the directional distribution of the current nondominated solutions in the
original many-objective space.

PAQC supplements this representative-based grouping signal with quality
information derived directly from the current nondominated solutions.
It constructs reference directions from their displacements relative to the
current estimated ideal point,
bypassing radial-grid representative selection, and uses PBI to assess evaluated
solutions along these directions. A stage-dependent fusion combines the
directional quality information with representative-based labels. Both signals
construct directions, associate solutions, and measure distances relative to
the same estimated ideal point.
The highest-ranked fraction forms the positive training group, and the remaining
solutions form its complement. PAQC thus retains REMO's representative-based
classification rule while adding another information source for relation-group construction.

Figure~\ref{fig:paqc-mechanism} provides a geometric overview of these two
signals and their roles in grouping.

For PAQC, $\mathcal{P}=\{(\vx_i,\vf_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ is the
current population, with $N=|\mathcal{P}|$. We estimate the ideal point as the
component-wise population minimum $\vz^{*}=\min_i\vf_i$ and define
$\vy_i=\vf_i-\vz^{*}$. Both PAQC signals use this common displacement
coordinate system, with $\vz^{*}$ as its origin. We translate the objective
vectors without rescaling individual objectives.

\subsubsection{Quality Assessment Using Nondominated-Derived Directions}
\label{sec:method:paqc:cont}

The current nondominated solutions provide the additional directional information.
Let $\{\vf^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{ND}}}$ denote the objective vectors of
the first nondominated front, and let
$\mathcal{J}=\{j\mid\|\vf^{(j)}-\vz^{*}\|_2>0\}$.
PAQC normalises their nonzero displacements to construct the direction set:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\vv_j&=\frac{\vf^{(j)}-\vz^{*}}{\|\vf^{(j)}-\vz^{*}\|_2},
\qquad j\in\mathcal{J},\\
\mathcal{V}&=\{\vv_j\mid j\in\mathcal{J}\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:direction}
\end{equation}
The number and orientations of these directions follow the current nondominated
distribution relative to $\vz^{*}$. Each direction defines a ray from $\vz^{*}$
through its source solution. This construction uses the nondominated solutions
directly, without radial-grid representative selection.
When the adaptive construction is not applicable, PAQC uses uniformly distributed
reference directions as a fallback. This includes $M\leq3$, $N<50$, too few
nondominated solutions, a degenerate objective range in the nondominated set,
or an empty $\mathcal{J}$.

For $\vy_i\ne\boldsymbol{0}$, PAQC associates the solution with the direction
of largest cosine similarity,
$a(i)=\arg\max_j\vy_i^{\mathsf T}\vv_j/\|\vy_i\|_2$.
All directions have unit length. The conventional
PBI value~\cite{ref:moead} and its bounded PAQC quality signal are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
d_{1,i}&=\vy_i^{\mathsf T}\vv_{a(i)},\\
d_{2,i}&=\|\vy_i-d_{1,i}\vv_{a(i)}\|_2,\\
g_i^{\mathrm{PBI}}&=d_{1,i}+\theta\,d_{2,i},\\
S_i&=\frac{1}{1+g_i^{\mathrm{PBI}}}\in(0,1],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pbi}
\end{equation}
where $\theta\geq0$ is the penalty parameter. A larger $S_i$ indicates a smaller
PBI value along the associated direction.

Direction construction, cosine association, and PBI evaluation all use
displacements from $\vz^{*}$. The population minimum gives
$\vy_i\geq\boldsymbol{0}$; both adaptive and uniform directions are
nonnegative, so $g_i^{\mathrm{PBI}}\geq0$ and $S_i\in(0,1]$.
For $\vy_i=\boldsymbol{0}$, PAQC assigns $d_{1,i}=d_{2,i}=0$ and $S_i=1$
directly, bypassing cosine association.

In the adaptive branch, each nondominated solution with nonzero displacement
aligns with its own direction in $\mathcal{V}$. Its perpendicular distance is
therefore zero, and \eqref{eq:pbi} reduces to
\begin{equation}
S_i=\frac{1}{1+\|\vf_i-\vz^{*}\|_2},
\qquad \vf_i\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{ND}}.
\label{eq:pbi-nd}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{ND}}$ denotes the current nondominated objective set.
On this set, $S_i$ ranks radial distance to $\vz^{*}$. For other solutions,
$d_{2,i}$ also penalises deviation from the associated direction.
The uniform-direction fallback uses the full PBI expression in \eqref{eq:pbi}.

\subsubsection{Representative-Based Grouping Signal}
\label{sec:method:paqc:coarse}

PAQC uses REMO's representative-based PBI classification rule~\cite{ref:remo}
within the same displacement coordinate system.
RSEA's radial-grid selection~\cite{ref:rsea} supplies a small set
$\mathcal{R}\subset\mathcal{P}$ of $k$ representative solutions from the current
population. These representatives supply the binary grouping signal. The
representatives are evaluated solutions that act as dynamic, population-derived
reference points. We set the requested representative count to
$k=\lceil1.5M\rceil$ in the considered experiments; the implementation bounds
are given in Section~\ref{sec:exp:settings}.

For each representative, define $\bar{\vr}_j=\vr_j-\vz^{*}$.
PAQC excludes zero-displacement representatives from directional association
and sets $\vw_j=\bar{\vr}_j/\|\bar{\vr}_j\|_2$ for the remaining ones.
For $\vy_i\ne\boldsymbol{0}$, it selects
$b(i)=\arg\max_j\vy_i^{\mathsf T}\vw_j/\|\vy_i\|_2$.
Using this associated direction, the two distances are
$\hat d_{1,i}=\vy_i^{\mathsf T}\vw_{b(i)}$ and
$\hat d_{2,i}=\|\vy_i-\hat d_{1,i}\vw_{b(i)}\|_2$.
The binary label is
\begin{equation}
L_i=\mathbb{I}\!\left[
\frac{\hat d_{1,i}+\delta\,\hat d_{2,i}}
     {\|\vr_{b(i)}-\vz^{*}\|_2}\leq1\right]\in\{0,1\}.
\label{eq:label-bin}
\end{equation}
Normalising by $\|\vr_{b(i)}-\vz^{*}\|_2$ makes the associated representative
itself the threshold, so $L_i$ records on which side of the surface through that
representative a solution lies. This normalisation requires
$\vr_{b(i)}\ne\vz^{*}$. PAQC assigns $L_i=1$ directly when
$\vy_i=\boldsymbol{0}$. If no representative has nonzero displacement,
it sets $L_i=\mathbb{I}[\|\vy_i\|_2=0]$ and bypasses directional classification.
Otherwise, bounded bisection adjusts the signed class-balance coefficient
$\delta\in[-20,20]$ toward a positive-label fraction in $[0.3,0.7]$,
stopping when it reaches that interval or the search bracket is narrower than
$0.1$. The target fraction need not be attainable in a degenerate population.

\subsubsection{Complementary-Signal Fusion for Positive-Group Construction}
\label{sec:method:paqc:hybrid}

The representative-based label describes membership relative to the selected
reference solutions, while the nondominated-derived directions supply additional
quality information relative to the same estimated ideal point. For fixed
solutions and representatives, both signals are invariant to a common additive
translation of the objective vectors because all calculations use their
displacements from $\vz^{*}$. With
$t=FE/FE_{\max}$, PAQC defines the hybrid quality score and the two groups as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
H_i&=(1-t)S_i+tL_i,\\
\mathcal{C}_1&=\bigl\{\vx_i\in\mathcal{P}\;\big|\;
  \operatorname{rank}_{\downarrow}(H_i)\leq\lceil N/4\rceil\bigr\},\\
\mathcal{C}_2&=\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{C}_1 .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:group}
\end{equation}
PAQC fixes the positive group to the top quarter of the population under $H_i$.
The compact positive group concentrates pairwise supervision on the
highest-ranked solutions, while $\mathcal{C}_2$ provides a broad contrast set.

Figure~\ref{fig:paqc-mechanism} illustrates the distinct roles of the two PBI
signals. In panel~(a), the representatives $R_1$ and $R_2$ define the local
thresholds used to obtain $L_i$. In panel~(b), the nondominated solutions
$P_1$ and $P_2$ supply the adaptive directions $\vv_1$ and $\vv_2$ through
\eqref{eq:direction}; these directions provide an additional directional
assessment through $S_i$. The fusion determines positive-group membership.
The reassignment of $A$ and $B$ to the positive
group and of $C$ to its complement schematically depicts how the additional
directional information can change selection priorities through
\eqref{eq:group}. PAQC therefore modifies the ranking used to form
the groups, without replacing the representative-based boundary with a new
geometric boundary.

The diagram uses two objectives to visualise the common ideal-point geometry.
The label $O=Z$ marks the origin of the displacement coordinates; it does not
assume that the estimated ideal point equals the origin of the original
objective coordinates. Both sets of rays start at $Z$, and the adaptive rays
pass through $P_1$ and $P_2$ by \eqref{eq:direction}.
The drawn PF serves only as an external visual reference: PAQC uses current
nondominated solutions to construct these directions. Their displayed
positive-group membership illustrates the fusion; nondominance alone does
not guarantee membership in $\mathcal{C}_1$.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:paqc} summarises the construction. Population order resolves
ties in $H_i$, giving exactly $\lceil N/4\rceil$ positive-group members.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{PBIQualityClassification (PAQC)}
\label{alg:paqc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require evaluated population $\mathcal{P}$, progress $t$
\Ensure groups $\mathcal{C}_1,\mathcal{C}_2$ and representatives $\mathcal{R}$
\State $\vz^{*}\gets\min_i\vf_i$; $\vy_i\gets\vf_i-\vz^{*}$
\State Construct $\mathcal{V}$ from nonzero nondominated displacements
\State Use uniform directions when the adaptive construction is unavailable
\State Associate nonzero $\vy_i$ with $\mathcal{V}$ by cosine similarity
\State Compute $S_i$ by \eqref{eq:pbi} for nonzero displacements
\State Assign $S_i\gets1$ at the ideal point
\State Select $k$ representatives $\mathcal{R}$ by radial-grid selection
\State Form representative directions from $\vr_j-\vz^{*}$
\State Associate nonzero $\vy_i$ with valid representative directions
\State Obtain $L_i$ by \eqref{eq:label-bin}, with the stated zero-displacement rules
\State Fuse the two signals: $H_i\gets(1-t)S_i+tL_i$
\State $\mathcal{C}_1\gets$ the top $\lceil|\mathcal{P}|/4\rceil$ solutions under $H_i$
\State $\mathcal{C}_2\gets\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{C}_1$
\State \Return $(\mathcal{C}_1,\mathcal{C}_2,\mathcal{R})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

By changing membership of $\mathcal{C}_1$, the fusion changes the ordered pairs
used to train the relation model. As $FE$ increases, group construction shifts
from directional quality toward representative-based membership; the
current-convergence analysis in Section~\ref{sec:exp:ggp} examines the resulting
positive groups.

\subsection{Relation Learning on the PAQC Groups}
\label{sec:method:relation}

PACDIS retains the relation model of REMO~\cite{ref:remo} and supplies it
with the groups constructed by PAQC. Every ordered pair
of distinct solutions becomes one training sample whose input is the
concatenation $[\vx_i,\vx_j]\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}$ and whose label
\begin{equation}
y_{ij}=
\begin{cases}
+1, & \vx_i\in\mathcal{C}_1,\ \vx_j\in\mathcal{C}_2,\\
-1, & \vx_i\in\mathcal{C}_2,\ \vx_j\in\mathcal{C}_1,\\
0,  & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:pairlabel}
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{C}_1,\mathcal{C}_2$ from \eqref{eq:group}, records the ordered
group relation rather than a Pareto comparison. Both same-group pair types
retain label~0 regardless of their members' $S_i$ or $H_i$ values. PAQC therefore
changes the composition of the training groups; the relation labels do not
encode quality ordering within either group. PAQC randomly subsamples
same-group pairs towards the size of each cross-group class to balance the
three labels of \eqref{eq:pairlabel}.

We employ the same relation-learning architecture as REMO: min--max normalised
pair inputs, a class-balanced training/held-out split, and a feed-forward pattern
network ending in a softmax over the three labels.
Its output for a pair is written
$\bm{\pi}(\vx_a,\vx_b)=[\pi_{+1},\pi_0,\pi_{-1}]$, the predicted probabilities
that the first solution belongs to the higher group, that both belong to the
same group, and that the second belongs to the higher group. The held-out
partition is retained by the training procedure; no held-out error is
used to gate candidate selection in the present implementation.

For an unevaluated candidate $\vx$ the trained network is queried on the four
families of ordered comparisons $(\mathcal{C}_1,\vx)$, $(\vx,\mathcal{C}_1)$,
$(\mathcal{C}_2,\vx)$ and $(\vx,\mathcal{C}_2)$, that is on $2N$ pairs per
candidate. With $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d}$ the family-wise
mean probability vectors in that order, the relation score aggregates the
evidence that $\vx$ is not inferior to the positive group and is superior to the
non-positive group, minus the opposite evidence,
\begin{equation}
R(\vx)=2\bigl[c_{-1}(\vx)+d_{+1}(\vx)-a_{+1}(\vx)-b_{-1}(\vx)\bigr]
\in[-4,4],
\label{eq:relscore}
\end{equation}
the bound following from each probability vector summing to one. $R(\vx)$ is the
aggregate group-relative preference used to order unevaluated candidates.

\subsection{Criterion-Diversified Infill Selection (CDIS)}
\label{sec:method:cdis}

PAQC determines the supervision supplied to the relation model, whereas CDIS
determines how the resulting surrogate information guides expensive-evaluation
allocation. CDIS uses two selection criteria: ambiguity-rewarded ranking
after relation-quality screening, and indicator-based reranking of a
relation-screened subset. The first criterion uses the relation model's
confidence information; the second uses a separate indicator surrogate.
A fixed cap bounds each expensive-evaluation batch.

Both modes use the same candidate-pool construction procedure. Genetic
variation is applied to the current population and representatives
$\mathcal{R}$; the relation score in \eqref{eq:relscore} ranks the offspring,
and up to $|\mathcal{R}|$ of the best are retained as parents of the next
inner generation together with $\mathcal{R}$. The search stops once the
cumulative number of generated candidates reaches $g_{\max}$. All generated
candidates are accumulated and deduplicated into $\mathcal{A}$. The mode is
drawn before this search: mode-specific aggregation of the pair predictions
can change the inner rankings and hence the realized pool. Thus, the modes
share a pool-construction procedure, rather than necessarily an identical pool.

\subsubsection{Ambiguity-Rewarded Infill}
\label{sec:method:cdis:exp}

The ambiguity-oriented mode first applies a relation-quality screen and then
ranks the retained candidates using predicted quality and classification
ambiguity. Its relation score uses the
largest softmax class probability as the weight of each ordered pair in the
family-wise averages of \eqref{eq:relscore}. This pair-level weighting gives
less influence to comparisons with lower maximum class probabilities when
estimating candidate quality.

Let $q=0.30$ be the nominal fraction retained by relation-quality screening
in either mode. Candidates whose relation scores are at or above the
$(1-q)$-quantile of $R(\mathcal{A})$ form $\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{amb}}$.
In the absence of ties, this screen retains approximately the highest-scoring
30\% of the pool; ties can increase that fraction. Ambiguity is considered only
after this quality screen, so a candidate cannot enter the batch solely because
of high predicted ambiguity.

To distinguish candidates within the retained set, let $\mathcal{O}(\vx)$
denote all ordered comparisons between candidate $\vx$ and the positive and
non-positive training groups. Let $\pi_k(\vu,\vv)$ be the predicted
probability of relation class $k\in\{-1,0,+1\}$ for an ordered pair
$(\vu,\vv)$. The candidate-level ambiguity and the acquisition score within
$\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{amb}}$ are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
U(\vx)&=1-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{O}(\vx)|}
  \sum_{(\vu,\vv)\in\mathcal{O}(\vx)}
  \max_{k\in\{-1,0,+1\}}\pi_k(\vu,\vv),\\
A_{\mathrm{amb}}(\vx)&=\widetilde{R}(\vx)+\lambda\,\widetilde{U}(\vx),
  \qquad \lambda=0.30.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:expscore}
\end{equation}
Here tildes denote min--max normalization within
$\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{amb}}$, with a constant quantity assigned the normalized
value $0.5$. Thus, ambiguity refines the relation-score ranking only among
candidates that pass the quality screen. The ambiguity-oriented branch returns
the first $n_b$ candidates in descending $A_{\mathrm{amb}}$ order, preserving
candidate order for exact ties.

\subsubsection{Indicator-Based Infill}
\label{sec:method:cdis:ind}

The indicator-oriented mode provides an alternative assessment of candidates
that pass relation-based screening. The quality indicator draws on the
modified SDE assessment in PIEA~\cite[Section~4.2, Eqs.~(7)--(10)]{ref:piea}.
Shift-based density estimation (SDE)~\cite{ref:sde} evaluates a solution
through distances to neighboring objective vectors after shifting each
neighbor's better objective components to the target solution's values.
The convergence component uses the negative $L_p$ distance to the current
ideal point, with larger values indicating better convergence.
PIEA estimates $p$ by minimizing the standard deviation of these distances
among current nondominated solutions, following the shape-estimation method
of TwoREA~\cite{ref:tworea}. PIEA combines shifted-distance assessment of
nondominated solutions with Minkowski convergence assessment of dominated
solutions and maps the scores to a common bounded scale.

For CDIS, the SDE and Minkowski convergence components supply the scalar
quality target $I_i$, with a finite convergence fallback. Larger values
indicate higher quality under the indicator. We compute $I_i$ for each solution in the
current population and train an
RBF-SVR, denoted by $\mathcal{M}_I$, on
$\mathcal{D}=\{(\vx_i,I_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$; its prediction is written
$\widehat I(\vx)=\mathcal{M}_I(\vx;\mathcal{D})$.
The relation score first retains the top
$\lceil q|\mathcal{A}|\rceil$ candidates to form $\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{ind}}$.
In this mode the family-wise relation probabilities are arithmetic averages.
The screened candidates are then ranked by descending $\widehat I$, and up to
$n_{\max}$ are selected directly. Failed or nonfinite indicator predictions
fall back to relation-score ranking. This branch separates candidate screening
from final quality assessment: relation predictions retain high-scoring
candidates, and the indicator surrogate ranks the screened subset.

\subsubsection{Selection-Criterion Switching}
\label{sec:method:cdis:mix}

Let $m\in\{\mathrm{amb},\mathrm{ind}\}$ denote the mode. With $u\sim U[0,1)$,
\begin{equation}
m=\begin{cases}
\mathrm{ind}, & \text{if the indicator surrogate is available}\\
              & \text{and }u<p_{\mathrm{mix}},\\[2pt]
\mathrm{amb}, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:mode}
\end{equation}
where $p_{\mathrm{mix}}=0.5$. One intact criterion is used per iteration;
the two scores need not be combined into a single ranking. This is a fixed
probability rule, with the ambiguity-oriented mode as the fallback when the indicator
surrogate is unavailable. Let $\mathcal{H}$ denote the retained set selected by
the current mode. The feasible batch size is
$n_b=\min(n_{\max},|\mathcal{H}|,FE_{\max}-FE)$, with $n_{\max}=6$.
A small retained set produces a smaller batch; it is not
completed to a prescribed minimum. If the pool is empty, genetic variation
provides fallback candidates, which are deduplicated and truncated to the same
cap and remaining budget. Algorithm~\ref{alg:cdis} states the main selection
procedure. The framework truncates any returned batch to the remaining budget
before its real evaluation and the corresponding $FE$ update.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{DiversifiedInfillSelection (CDIS)}
\label{alg:cdis}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require population $\mathcal{P}$, representatives $\mathcal{R}$,
         surrogates $\mathcal{M}_R,\mathcal{M}_I$, mode $m$
\Ensure ordered candidate batch $\mathcal{S}$
\State Run relation-guided variation from $\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{R}$ using mode $m$
\State $\mathcal{A}\gets$ the accumulated, deduplicated candidate pool
\If{$m=\mathrm{ind}$}
  \State $\mathcal{H}\gets$ top $q$ fraction by $R$
  \State Rerank $\mathcal{H}$ by $\widehat I$, falling back to $R$ if needed
\Else
  \State $\mathcal{H}\gets\{\,\vx\in\mathcal{A}:R(\vx)\geq Q_{1-q}(R(\mathcal{A}))\,\}$
  \State Compute $A_{\mathrm{amb}}$ within $\mathcal{H}$ by \eqref{eq:expscore}
\EndIf
\State $n_b\gets\min(n_{\max},|\mathcal{H}|,FE_{\max}-FE)$
\State $\mathcal{S}\gets$ the first $n_b$ candidates in $\mathcal{H}$ under the selected ranking
\State \Return $\mathcal{S}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Computational Complexity}
\label{sec:method:complexity}

Let $N$ be the population size, $N_v=O(N)$ the number of directions, $N_c$ the
size of the deduplicated candidate pool, $D$ the number of decision variables
and $M$ the number of objectives.

In PAQC, association and the continuous PBI computation
\eqref{eq:pbi} cost $O(NN_vM)$, the direction set
\eqref{eq:direction} costs one normalisation of the nondominated vectors, the
bounded bisection of \eqref{eq:label-bin} costs $O(B_\delta NkM)$ with
$B_\delta$ fixed by the bracket and the tolerance, and representative selection
adds nondominated sorting and radial-projection distances. With $N_v=O(N)$ and
$k=O(N)$ the module is dominated by $O(N^2M)$, and generating the relation pairs
adds $O(N^2D)$.

In CDIS, scoring one candidate requires $2N$ network inputs of
dimension $2D$. Constructing the inputs for the pool costs $O(N_cND)$.
The relation network has a fixed number of dense layers with hidden-layer widths
proportional to $D$, so predicting one ordered pair costs $O(D^2)$.
Relation prediction over the pool therefore costs $O(N_cND^2)$.
The indicator fitness costs $O(N^2M)$, and the indicator-oriented mode adds
one SVR fit on the $N$ evaluated solutions plus predictions on the screened
subset. Sorting the ambiguity-rewarded acquisition
costs $O(N_c\log N_c)$.
The corresponding cost, excluding surrogate fitting, is
$O(N_cND^2+N^2M+N_c\log N_c)$.
Neither module directly consumes expensive evaluations. The remaining-budget
check ensures that PACDIS does not exceed $FE_{\max}$, regardless of the mode sequence.

\subsection{Experimental Settings}
\label{sec:exp:settings}

\paragraph{Test problems and evaluation budget.}
The main performance study covers DTLZ1--DTLZ7 and WFG1--WFG9 with
$M\in\{10,15,20\}$, giving 48 problem--objective configurations. These
settings target expensive optimization with relatively large numbers of
objectives. The main comparison uses PlatEMO~\cite{ref:platemo},
with a configured population size of $N=100$ and a maximum budget of
$FE_{\max}=500$ real function evaluations, including initialization.
The decision dimension was $D=30$, except for WFG2 and WFG3 at
$M=10$ and $M=20$, where the problem definitions required $D=31$.
All 15-objective instances used $D=30$.

\paragraph{Compared algorithms.}
PACDIS is compared with six surrogate-assisted algorithms:
REMO~\cite{ref:remo}, SSDE~\cite{ref:ssde}, PC-SAEA~\cite{ref:pcsaea},
SAMOEA-TL2M~\cite{ref:samoeatl2m}, CSEA~\cite{ref:csea}, and
HES-EA~\cite{ref:hesea}. REMO learns pairwise relations, SSDE uses a
self-organizing surrogate, and PC-SAEA uses pairwise comparison. SAMOEA-TL2M
uses two-level model management, CSEA uses classification, and HES-EA combines
hierarchical and ensemble surrogates with model reduction. The configured
population size $N$ does not specify a common initialization count across
these implementations.

\paragraph{PACDIS parameters.}
The same configuration is used across all three objective counts:
$g_{\max}=3000$, $p_{\mathrm{mix}}=0.50$, and the nominal relation-screening
fraction $q=0.30$. The fixed internal constants are the ambiguity weight
$\lambda=0.30$ and continuous-PBI penalty $\theta=5$.
The requested representative count is
\begin{equation}
k_{\mathrm{eff}}=\min\{N,\max(6,\lceil1.5M\rceil)\},
\label{eq:keff}
\end{equation}
giving 15, 23 and 30 at $M=10$, 15 and 20 with $N=100$.
The actual returned representative count can be smaller.
No error-gate threshold or minimum-batch
parameter is used in this version.

\paragraph{Performance indicator and statistical reporting.}
Performance is measured by inverted generational distance plus (IGD$^+$), with
smaller values indicating a better approximation to the reference Pareto
front. Tables~\ref{tab:exp:dtlz} and~\ref{tab:exp:wfg} group the results by
problem, with three rows for $M=10$, 15 and 20. Each entry reports the mean
above the standard deviation in parentheses. The cell with the lowest mean
among the seven retained algorithms has a light gray background. The pairwise
statistical annotations come from the supplied workbooks. A $+$ indicates
that the baseline was reported as better than PACDIS, a $-$ indicates that it
was reported as worse, and an $=$ indicates no detected difference. The bottom
row of each table counts these symbols in the order $+/-/=$ for each baseline
across all problem--objective configurations in that table. These counts
summarize the supplied pairwise outcomes and are not a separate omnibus test
across problems.

Each table entry summarizes 20 independent runs with identifiers 1--20,
following the final experiment records supplied by the authors.
The export script applies an unpaired rank-sum test at $p<0.05$ without a
multiple-comparison adjustment. We retain its symbols without interpreting
them as adjusted significance.
\paragraph{Reference sets and execution environment.}
We use PlatEMO's default DTLZ and WFG problem definitions with the $M$ and $D$
settings above. PlatEMO constructs each reference set through the problem's
\texttt{GetOptimum(10000)} method; the returned number of points depends on
the problem and objective count. The \texttt{IGDp} implementation computes
distances in the original objective space without additional normalization.
Most runs use MATLAB R2023a on a Windows workstation with an Intel Core
i5-12400 processor and 32 GB RAM. Six parallel workers execute independent
optimization runs.

using either the hybrid score $H$ or the representative margin $A$.
We define $A$ as one minus
the normalized representative-based PBI value. This continuous margin provides an equal-quota
representative control. Both rankings resolve ties by population order.

% Outcome: assess convergence within each current population.
We compute a retrospective convergence diagnostic from the saved decision
vectors. The 25 solutions with the smallest DTLZ convergence quantity $g$
form the reference set $\mathcal{Y}^{+}$ at each checkpoint.
Here, $g=\sum_{j=M}^{D}(x_j-0.5)^2$ for DTLZ2, DTLZ4, and DTLZ5, and
$g=1+9\sum_{j=M}^{D}x_j/(D-M+1)$ for DTLZ7.
For a selected positive group $\mathcal{C}$, we measure current convergence
precision through its good-group precision (GGP) against this reference set:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{GGP}=\frac{|\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{Y}^{+}|}{|\mathcal{C}|}.
\label{eq:ggp}
\end{equation}
This diagnostic measures convergence enrichment,
with a fixed chance level of 25\%. DTLZ7 also requires the appropriate
location among its disconnected Pareto regions. All checkpoints have
distinct $g$ values at the selection boundary. The diagnostic reuses saved
trajectories and adds zero real evaluations.

We first average the 34 checkpoints within each run, then summarize the
20 independent runs per configuration. Table~\ref{tab:exp:ggp} reports
means and standard deviations. Two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests
compare PAQC with the representative-margin control.
The eight reported tests form a subset of the original 32-test family,
which spans two outcomes, two controls, and eight configurations.
We retain the original Holm-adjusted $p$-values at $\alpha=0.05$;
the reduced presentation leaves the correction family unchanged.
Overall summaries give equal weight to each configuration.

% Convergence enrichment: a large, reproducible effect against the named control.
PAQC more than doubles convergence precision relative to the
representative-margin control: 73.68\% versus 35.83\%, a 2.06-fold ratio.
The gain reaches 37.85 percentage points overall and ranges from 26.98 to
46.62 points across the eight configurations; every comparison passes
Holm correction. At the same 25-solution quota, this corresponds to
9.46 additional low-$g$ solutions per positive group on average.

% Takeaway: bound the evidence to current positive-group convergence.
These matched-checkpoint results show that PAQC concentrates more low-$g$
solutions in its positive training group than representative-margin grouping
at the same quota. The comparison measures current positive-group convergence
along PACDIS trajectories. The IGD$^+$ experiments assess the quality of
the resulting approximation sets.

\subsection{Effect of Selection-Criterion Switching}
\label{sec:exp:pmix}

Equation~\eqref{eq:mode} sets $p_{\mathrm{mix}}=0.5$ as the default.
Table~\ref{tab:exp:pmix} compares five settings in the NoBatchDist
configuration on six problems at each of $M=10$ and 20. Each cell uses
20 runs and reports final IGD$^+$. Across the 12 problem--objective
configurations, $p_{\mathrm{mix}}=0.50$ has the lowest average rank, 2.08;
$p_{\mathrm{mix}}=0.75$ follows at 2.33. The default does not lead on every
problem: $p_{\mathrm{mix}}=0$ improves DTLZ7 at $M=10$, and
$p_{\mathrm{mix}}=0.25$ improves DTLZ7 at $M=20$, according to the paired
tests in the source table. At $p_{\mathrm{mix}}=1$, the algorithm can still
use the ambiguity-oriented mode when the indicator surrogate is unavailable.
The results support $0.50$ as the best-ranked setting in this tested subset.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
PACDIS modifies supervision construction and expensive-evaluation allocation
in relation-based surrogate-assisted optimization. PAQC combines
representative-based grouping with directional quality information obtained
from the current nondominated set to construct positive training groups.
CDIS allocates expensive evaluations through ambiguity-oriented and
indicator-oriented candidate-selection criteria after relation-quality
screening. The REMO-referenced comparisons favor PACDIS on most tested
configurations, with reversals on WFG3. The exported symbols compare each
configuration with REMO, while the Full-versus-control mean differences
characterize the effects of the module deletions. PAQC and CDIS address
complementary interfaces by design, with problem-dependent performance effects.
The matched group analysis shows that PAQC raises mean current convergence
precision from 35.83\% to 73.68\% relative to equal-quota
representative-margin grouping on the four tested DTLZ problems
(Section~\ref{sec:exp:ggp}).
